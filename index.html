Why Supplements Have Different Pill Coatings

Pick up any two supplement bottles and the pills inside may look nothing alike: one glossy and smooth, another matte and chalky. That coating is not decoration — it does several jobs at once.

The most basic job is swallowing. A plain compressed tablet has rough edges that catch in the throat; a thin film coating makes it glide down. That is why some brands list "vegetable glaze" or "pharmaceutical glaze" in the other-ingredients line — it is simply a swallow aid.

The second job is protection. Moisture breaks tablets down, and a coating slows that down. Some coatings also mask unpleasant tastes — iron and certain botanicals are notorious for it.

The third job is timing. Enteric coatings are designed to survive stomach acid and dissolve in the intestine instead. Whether a given formula actually needs that is a formulation choice, but the coating itself is a legitimate technology, not a trick.

What should raise an eyebrow? Coatings that exist to make a tablet look premium while adding synthetic dyes with no functional purpose — check the "other ingredients" line to see what the coating actually is.
